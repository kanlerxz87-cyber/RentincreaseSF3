How Landlords Handle Increases — Corporations vs. Individual Owners

What the research shows about increase behavior — clearly separated into official rules (binding on everyone) and third-party research (context, not law). No row below invents a number; where a figure could not be confirmed it is marked unverified and mirrored on Flagged for Review.

Ground rule: In a rent-controlled SF unit, owner sophistication is irrelevant to the amount. A REIT, a corporate manager, and a mom-and-pop owner face the identical 1.6% ceiling and the identical banking rules. Practice differs in how consistently they max it out, document it, and automate notices — not in what they may charge.

C. How “normal” increases actually play out in practice

Large corporate / institutional owners

  • Systematic annual maximization. Allowable % applied every year by default via property-management software; banking applied automatically when anniversaries pass.
  • Strict documentation. Auditable ledgers, templated notices, tracked service — designed to survive Rent Board petitions and lawyer scrutiny.
  • Uniform policy language. One written policy across portfolio (uniform formula ⇒ discrimination defense by design).
  • Fast escalation. Full utilizations + petitions (O&M, capital passthroughs) whenever expenses outpace the allowable rate.
  • Distant relationships — more aggressive on eviction filing; same research shows higher asking rents after acquisitions.
  • What they cannot do: exceed the SF allowable/banked amounts; skip the license; skip notice. Rent control binds the big players hardest in practice because they run the biggest portfolios through the same Rent Board.

Individual / “mom-and-pop” owners in SF

  • Often skip annual increases (under-utilized banking) — exactly the client’s posture (Unit 2: ~7 unused anniversaries). Common, lawful, and recoverable later via banking.
  • Relationship-priced. Increases at or below allowable; goodwill with long-term tenants; irregular timing off anniversaries is where mistakes happen (new anniversary created, riper banked increases lost).
  • Paperwork gaps. Missing proof of service or license lapses — the two most common failure points when a tenant challenges an increase; also the two things this client must fix before filing anything.
  • Same legal ceiling as corporations; typically less sophisticated documentation — which is why the client’s checklist on the Notice page exists.

D. Why “normal” for these two units is the allowable increase

San Francisco’s regime makes “normal business practice” legally legible: the Rent Board publishes the allowable amount each year (60% of Bay Area CPI), capped at 7%, and the only lawful excess routes through petitions. For a 1940 two-unit building with long-term tenants, a ≤3.0% (Unit 1) or ≤13.1% banked catch-up (Unit 2, >10% ⇒ 90-day notice) is textbook normal. Anything larger absent a petition or vacancy would be self-evidently abnormal to the Rent Board, to Bay Area Legal Aid, or to the SF Tenants Union — the exact audiences the client asked this analysis to survive.
