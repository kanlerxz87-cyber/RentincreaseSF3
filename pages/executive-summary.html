1. The Rent Board question — do I need a license? (read first)

Yes — even as an individual, non-corporate property owner. There is no small-landlord or individual-owner exemption. Under the San Francisco Rent Ordinance (Administrative Code § 37.15):

  • You must report to the Rent Board. Every owner of residential property must report each unit into the Rent Board's Housing Inventory online through the Rent Board Portal (portal.sfrb.org). Reporting is free and due every March 1; for buildings with 1–9 residential units (like this two-unit building) the requirement has applied to rent increases effective on or after March 1, 2023.
  • You must hold a rent increase license. Once you report a tenant-occupied unit, a rent increase license is automatically generated. A landlord must have a current rent increase license on file with the Rent Board before any annual or banked rent increase can go into effect. The license does not need to be included with the tenant notice, but it must exist on the increase's effective date — without it the increase is null and void.
  • This is how you "inform the rent board." The Housing Inventory report is your notification to the Rent Board. The Rent Board does not notify your tenants for you: you serve the tenant a written notice yourself (step 4 below), and the increase takes effect only after the notice period runs and the license is in place.

Official sources: sf.gov — The Rent Board Housing Inventory · sf.gov — Learn about rent increases in San Francisco · sf.gov — Ordinance 265-20 (license requirement)

5. Raise one unit, or both?

Neither the Rent Ordinance nor fair-housing law requires equal percentage increases, and raising only one unit is not unlawful by itself. Unlawful treatment arises when a difference is tied to a protected characteristic (Cal. Gov. Code §12955; 42 U.S.C. §3604) or to retaliation (Civ. Code §1942.5). The defensible pattern is one written rule, applied to both units on the same schedule, with each unit's rent history documented before notice is served. Different outcomes that follow from seven skipped years versus two are explainable; applying different rules to similarly placed tenants is what invites discrimination inquiries or lawsuits.

Sources: leginfo — Gov. Code §12955 · govinfo — 42 U.S.C. §3604 · sf.gov — discrimination → SF Human Rights Commission

6. Scrutiny readiness — built to pass review by a San Francisco tenant lawyer

Tenants in San Francisco can obtain free legal help (Eviction Defense Collaborative, 415-659-9184; Rent Board counseling, 415-252-4600) and may file a no-fee tenant petition, including challenging an invalid rent-increase notice. An increase built this way is designed to pass that review:

  • Coverage confirmed (pre-6/13/1979 building) — sf.gov
  • Current rent increase license on file before the effective date — sf.gov — Housing Inventory
  • Correct rate for the effective date (not the service date); base rent only; no rounding up — sf.gov
  • Once-per-12-months limit respected; banked math added, not compounded — sf.gov — banked increases
  • Written 30/90-day notice stating dollar amount, percentage, effective date, and banked breakdown — Civ. Code §827 · sf.gov
  • No charge for additional occupants; no reason tied to a protected characteristic, complaint, repair request, or new roommate — sf.gov
  • Same written policy for both units; differences traceable to rent history — Gov. Code §12955 · 42 U.S.C. §3604
