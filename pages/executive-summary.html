Executive summary

Prepared November 6, 2026 for two rent-controlled units in the Outer Sunset, San Francisco 94122, in a building reported built in 1940. All tenant names and unit designations are pseudonymised; all dates and dollar amounts are exactly as supplied.

Bottom line

Unit A: raise rent 3.0% — $119.85, taking base rent from $3,995.00 to $4,114.85 — effective February 1, 2027, with a 30-day written notice served on or before January 2, 2027 (December 28, 2026 if mailed).

Unit B: raise rent by the verified banked total of 13.1% plus the annual rate then in effect, worth at least $412.65, taking base rent from $3,150.00 to at least $3,562.65, effective June 1, 2027, with a 90-day written notice served on or before March 3, 2027 (February 26, 2027 if mailed). The rate for the period beginning March 1, 2027 had not been published as of the analysis date and is left blank rather than forecast.

Raise both units, each to its own lawful amount, on each unit’s own anniversary date. Do not attempt to equalise the percentages — that would exceed Unit A’s ceiling and void the increase entirely.

What is “normal”, measured from the official table. The mean allowable annual increase since March 1, 2019 is 1.96% (median 1.75%, n=8); since April 1, 1982 it is 2.36% (median 1.9%, n=44). A normal annual increase in San Francisco is therefore roughly 1.5–2.5%, and 1.6% right now. Unit A’s recommended 3.0% sits in that band. Unit B’s 13.1%+ is lawful but is a one-step recovery of seven years of forbearance, and the official rules expressly permit taking less. Source: S2 · S4 · S5

The constitutional floor on the other side. California’s Legislative Analyst records that courts have ruled rent control must allow landlords to receive a fair rate of return. Taking nothing for seven years is not required; taking the published rate is. Source: S32

2. How do large corporations and individual owners handle this?

The legal ceiling is identical. Three verifiable differences matter:

  • Entity form narrows corporate exemptions. Civ. Code Sec. 1947.12(d)(5)(A) denies the separately-alienable (single-family / condominium) exemption to a REIT, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member. Source: S24
  • Reporting deadlines arrived earlier for large portfolios. The rent increase license requirement became operative July 1, 2022 for non-condominium buildings with 10 or more units, and reporting began March 1, 2023 for condominiums and buildings with fewer than 10. A two-unit owner therefore has less institutional muscle and a later, tighter deadline — but the same consequence if the license is missing. Source: S8 · S9
  • Algorithmic pricing is banned outright. Rent Ordinance Sec. 37.10C (Ordinance 224-24, effective October 14, 2024) prohibits the sale or use of algorithmic devices to set rents or manage occupancy levels for residential units. The revenue-management software standard in institutional multifamily is unlawful in San Francisco — for corporations and for individual owners alike. Source: S6

What the corporate record does show, from SEC filings by a large Bay Area multifamily REIT, is that institutional owners describe rent control as something that ‘limit[s] our ability to charge market rents, increase rents, evict tenants or recover increases in our operating expenses’, and that they manage to portfolio revenue targets through annual renewal cycles. Neither a portfolio target nor a renewal cycle is a lawful ground for an increase here: the only grounds are the closed list in Notice procedure & reasons. Source: S37 · S38 · S13

3. How do you notify a tenant correctly?

  1. Confirm coverage, base rent and the complete rent history.
  2. Confirm a current rent increase license is on file for the effective date (Rent Ordinance Sec. 37.15). Without it the entire increase is null and void. Source: S8 · S9 · S6
  3. Compute on base rent only: matured banked percentages added — never compounded — plus the annual rate in effect on the effective date, truncated to the cent, never rounded up. Source: S3 · S5
  4. Give written notice: 30 days if the total is 10% or less, 90 days if more than 10%, plus 5 days if mailed. Source: S23 · S3
  5. State the dollar amount, the percentage, the effective date, and which portion is banked and from what dates, plus any certified or passthrough component on the Board’s forms. Source: S6 · S5
  6. Keep the notice, the proof of service and the worksheet.

Legitimate reasons are a closed list: the published annual allowable increase; properly matured banked increases; a Board-certified capital improvement, operating and maintenance expense, comparable-units or Proposition I petition; utility, water revenue bond and general obligation bond passthroughs on the Board’s worksheets; lead hazard remediation; and recovery of 50% of the Rent Board fee ($29.50 per dwelling unit for 2026-2027). Anything beyond that requires a Sec. 37.8 petition before the notice is served. Source: S13 · S6 · S10

Reasons that will fail: market rate while an original occupant remains; a new or replacement occupant (Sec. 37.3(a)(11)(A) applies even if the lease permits it); costs rising without a certified petition; the state 8.8% cap; the lease saying so; a pricing model; and any retaliatory or discriminatory motive. Source: S6 · S3 · S11 · S24

4. Raise one unit, or both?

Both, each to its own lawful amount, under one written policy. Raising only one is lawful — the Ordinance is unit- and tenancy-specific, and there is no requirement to raise every unit. But the difference here (Unit A 3.0% against Unit B 13.1%+) is produced entirely by the official rate table applied to each unit’s own anniversary date and tenancy length: a facially neutral, documentable, statute-driven basis with no connection to any protected characteristic. That is the answer to a discrimination inquiry, and it must be documented before the decision. Source: S2 · S5 · S33 · S34

The trap is the intuitive one. Making the percentages equal would exceed Unit A’s ceiling and, under Sec. 37.3(b)(5), render the increase null and void in its entirety. Source: S6

Residual exposure to manage on Unit B: a step increase above 10% requires 90 days’ notice and is the fact pattern most often challenged. California protects age, disability, familial status and source of income, and a tenant may file a Tenant Financial Hardship Application (Form 363). Neither occupant’s age, disability status or income source was supplied — left blank. Taking less than the maximum is expressly permitted and materially reduces this exposure. Source: S33 · S14 · S4

5. Would it survive a San Francisco tenant lawyer?

Conditionally yes. Fifteen attack vectors are modelled on the red team page. Most are low risk on the facts as supplied. Three conditions must be closed first:

  1. The rent increase license. Unknown for both units; highest residual risk in the file because it is independent of the arithmetic. Source: S8 · S9
  2. The banking maturity conflict. Two official sf.gov pages disagree — 12 months (Rules and Regulations Sec. 4.12(a), controlling) versus 24 months (the summary page). Confirm with a Rent Board counselor on 415-252-4600 before serving a banked-increase notice. Source: S5 · S4
  3. The paper file. Base-rent composition, prior increase history, Rent Board fee receipts and deposit-interest records were not supplied and are left blank. Source: S3 · S10 · S1
