Raise one unit, or both?

The question is whether a rent increase taken on one unit but not the other creates discrimination exposure. The answer turns on a single legal test: why the two units were treated differently. Treating two units differently is not itself unlawful. Treating them differently because of a protected characteristic is.

The counter-intuitive finding: making the increases EQUAL would be unlawful

It is tempting to think that fairness means raising both units by the same percentage. On these facts that would be the unlawful option. Unit A’s lawful ceiling is 3.0%; Unit B’s banked entitlement alone is 13.1% before the then-current annual rate is added. Raising Unit A to anything above 3.0% would exceed the amount permitted by Sec. 37.3 and, under Sec. 37.3(b)(5), the entire increase would be null and void — with a tenant refund claim behind it. Equal percentages are not achievable here without breaking the law on one unit or the other. Source: S6 · S5 · S2

Applying the test to these two units

Applying the test to these two units
Question Analysis Conclusion Source
Do the two units have different lawful ceilings? Yes, and by a wide margin. Unit A: 3.0%. Unit B: 13.1% banked plus the then-current annual rate. The difference is produced entirely by the official document 571 rate table applied to each unit’s own anniversary date and tenancy length. Documented objective basis Source: S2 · S5 · S4
Is that difference tied to any protected characteristic? No. Tenancy start date and the number of skipped anniversaries are neutral, documentable facts recorded in the lease and in the Rent Board’s own rate table. Nothing in either dossier links the difference to race, colour, ancestry, national origin, citizenship, immigration status, primary language, religion, disability, sex or gender, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, genetic information, marital status, familial status, source of income, military or veteran status, or age. No protected basis Source: S33 · S34
Is either increase retaliatory? No retaliatory motive was reported. Note that retaliation for a tenant filing a complaint, requesting an accommodation, or otherwise protecting their rights is expressly unlawful in California, and that the Rent Board cannot hear the claim — so this is a real exposure if facts emerge. None reported — blank, unverified — Source: S33 · S14
Would raising only one unit be unlawful? No. San Francisco’s regime is inherently unit- and tenancy-specific: each unit has its own base rent, its own anniversary date and its own banked pool. There is no requirement to raise every unit in a building, and no prohibition on raising only one. Lawful Source: S3 · S5 · S4
Would raising only one unit be risky? It can be. If the two units’ households differ on any protected characteristic and only one is increased, a complainant will ask why. The answer must be documented before the decision, not constructed afterwards. Manageable with a written policy Source: S33 · S34
Does Unit B’s large increase carry its own risk? Yes, of a different kind. A step increase of more than 10% is the fact pattern most often challenged, it requires a 90-day notice, and if either occupant is elderly, disabled, on a fixed income or a voucher holder, a disparate-impact or hardship argument becomes available. California protects age, disability, familial status and source of income. Tenants may file a Tenant Financial Hardship Application (Form 363). Neither occupant’s age, disability status or income source was supplied. — blank, unverified — Unknown — must be checked Source: S33 · S14 · S6
Can a housing service be reduced instead of raising rent? No. Parking, driveways, storage, laundry rooms, decks, patios and gardens supplied with a unit may not be severed without just cause, and any severance, substantial reduction or removal of a housing service must be offset by a corresponding reduction in rent. A reduction in housing services without a corresponding rent reduction is itself defined as a rent increase. Do not do this Source: S6

Recommended approach

One written policy, applied to both units, each to its own lawful limit
  1. Adopt a single written rent-increase policy covering both units: the same annual review date, the same calculation method, the same notice format, and the same stated criteria for deciding whether to take the full entitlement or less. Apply it identically.
  2. Raise both units, each to its own lawful amount, on each unit’s own anniversary date. Unit A to 3.0% on February 1, 2027; Unit B to 13.1% plus the then-current annual rate on June 1, 2027. Identical process, different lawful outcomes — which is what the Ordinance requires.
  3. Document the objective basis in the file before serving: tenancy start date, complete rent history, the applicable document 571 rates, the calculation worksheet, and the license confirmation. The justification must predate the notice.
  4. Do not equalise percentages across units. That would exceed Unit A’s ceiling and void the increase.
  5. Check for a hardship or disparate-impact factor on Unit B before choosing the full 13.1%. If either occupant is elderly, disabled, on a fixed income or a voucher holder, a phased increase materially reduces exposure and the official rules expressly permit taking less than the maximum. Do not use any of that information as a reason to treat a unit differently — use it only to moderate the amount.
  6. Never link the decision to a protected characteristic, to a complaint, or to an accommodation request. Retaliation is expressly unlawful under California law and is outside the Rent Board’s jurisdiction.
  7. Do not use algorithmic pricing. Sec. 37.10C prohibits it for every owner.
Source: S33 · S34 · S6 · S4 · S14
