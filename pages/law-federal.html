Federal law

Federal law does not set a rent-increase ceiling for privately owned, unsubsidised residential housing. What it does impose is a constraint on how rent may be set: the Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in the terms, conditions or privileges of a rental, including price. Federal rent limits attach only to federally assisted housing.

Fair Housing Act protected characteristics

Federal protected characteristics
Characteristic Notes from the DOJ statement
Race or colour A central objective of the Act since 1968; most DOJ pattern-or-practice cases involve race discrimination. DOJ notes that providers sometimes disguise discrimination with false information about availability, or by steering.
Religion Covers overt discrimination and less direct actions. A limited exception exists for non-commercial housing operated by a religious organisation.
Sex Includes sexual harassment; DOJ’s enforcement targets landlords who create an untenable living environment by demanding sexual favours or creating a sexually hostile environment.
National origin May be based on the individual’s country of birth or that of their ancestors.
Familial status Families with children and pregnancy.
Disability Includes the duty to permit reasonable modifications and reasonable accommodations.

What this means in practice

Federal law is not a source of permission to raise rent — it is a source of constraint. A lawful, correctly calculated San Francisco increase can still be unlawful federally and under state law if the reason for it, or the pattern of who receives it, is tied to a protected characteristic. Critically, the Rent Board cannot hear these claims: it states that it does not adjudicate harassment, discrimination or retaliation, and that such matters must be decided in court or another forum. That routes any discrimination allegation to the California Civil Rights Department, to HUD, or to court — a different forum with different remedies from a rent-overcharge petition.
