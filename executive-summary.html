Executive summary

The decision, the numbers, and the risks - on one page.

Purpose

The owner of two flats in a 1940 building in San Francisco's Outer Sunset (ZIP 94122) asked what a reasonable and normal rent increase looks like, whether it would survive review by a tenant-side advocate or lawyer, and whether to raise one unit or both. This site records the official-source answer, the arithmetic, the notice procedure, and the open items.

1. The ceiling is set locally, and it is small

Both units are rent-controlled on the owner's description: the building predates 13 June 1979. Because a tenant from each original tenancy remains in possession, no vacancy has occurred, so neither rent can be reset to market. The allowable annual increase is set each 1 March at 60% of the prior year's Bay Area CPI change, capped at 7%.

  • 3/1/2026 - 2/28/2027: 1.6% S1 S4 S7
  • 3/1/2025 - 2/28/2026: 1.4% S1 S4
  • Average of the last eight published years: 1.96% (2.6, 1.8, 0.7, 2.3, 3.6, 1.7, 1.4, 1.6) S4

The statewide Tenant Protection Act is not the operative rule: it caps increases at 5% + CPI (8.8% for this region for 1 Aug 2026 - 31 Jul 2027), but it exempts housing already subject to a local ordinance that restricts increases to a lower amount. S16 S21 No federal statute sets a rent ceiling; the federal rule that does apply to increase decisions is the Fair Housing Act's ban on discriminatory terms. S20

2. Skipped years can be added, subject to a 24-month condition

If the owner does not take an increase, the unused amount can be banked and taken later. The Rent Board's current guidance requires at least 24 full months without any rent increase before a skipped increase is treated as banked; percentages are added, never compounded, and never rounded up. There is no ceiling on the banked total, and banked amounts do not expire during the tenancy. S3 S8 S14

3. Unit A - $3,995, tenancy from 1 Feb 2025

The first increase could not be imposed before 1 Feb 2026 (1.4%), and none was imposed. On 1 Feb 2027 the owner is 24 months without an increase, so both years are available: 1.4% + 1.6% = 3.0% = $119.85, taking base rent to $4,114.85. A 30-day notice is enough because 3.0% is well under 10%.

The 1 April 2026 renewal, at which a second tenant joined the original tenant, does not create a vacancy and does not allow extra rent: San Francisco prohibits additional rent solely because an occupant moved in. S2 S13 S17

Conservative fallback: if a counselor reads the 24-month condition narrowly, the current 1.6% alone is $63.92, taking rent to $4,058.92. S2

4. Unit B - $3,150, tenancy from 1 Jun 2019, no increase since

Seven anniversary increases have been skipped, and the 24-month condition is met many times over. The percentages in effect on each anniversary (June 2020 through June 2026) are 1.8, 0.7, 2.3, 3.6, 1.7, 1.4 and 1.6: 13.1% = $412.65, taking base rent to $3,562.65. Because the increase exceeds 10%, a 90-day notice is required. S3 S4 S15

Two cautions. First, the 1.6% year ends 28 Feb 2027, so a notice relying on it must be served by 30 Nov 2026. Second, a pending ordinance (File 261020) would cap combined banked and capital-improvement increases at 10% per year; it is not law yet, but an increase of exactly 10% ($315.00, to $3,465.00) is lawful today, would remain lawful if the cap passes, and leaves $97.65 banked. S22 S3

5. Reasonable and normal

For a rent-controlled San Francisco unit, "reasonable and normal" means: the published annual percentage, applied on the anniversary date, with correct notice and a current licence. That is what the ordinance contemplates and what the Rent Board's own examples show. A banked catch-up is also lawful, but it is the practice that tenant advocates and the Board of Supervisors are actively targeting, it draws petitions, and after the pending cap it may be limited to 10% a year. There is no legal requirement to raise rent at all, and no requirement that both units be raised.

6. One unit or both?

Nothing in the ordinance or in fair-housing law requires equal dollar increases. Raising only one unit is not unlawful by itself; unlawful treatment arises when the difference is tied to a protected characteristic or to retaliation. The defensible pattern is to adopt one written rule (for example: "the annual allowable increase, or the banked total if 24 months have passed"), apply it to both units on the same schedule, and document the tenancy-history reason for any difference. Applying different rules to similarly placed tenants is what invites inquiry. S19 S20 S18

7. What must still be verified

  • Property record (year built, unit count, use) - coverage is argued from the owner's statement. S5
  • Housing Inventory report and current rent increase licence. S6
  • Prior notices, leases and proofs of service for both units. S11
  • Status of the pending 10% cap. S22
  • The 12-vs-24-month banking discrepancy between two official pages. S3 S14
