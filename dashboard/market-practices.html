How corporations vs individual owners handle rent increases

Answered strictly from official sources. Where no official data exists, the gap is stated plainly and flagged instead of filled in.

Baseline (official): the rules are identical for every owner. The Rent Ordinance's annual/banked limits, notice rules, license requirement and petition process apply to corporate and individual owners alike — “All owners of residential property are required to report” into the Housing Inventory, and all covered tenancies share the same rate table. Source: sf.gov — Housing Inventory, sf.gov — Learn about rent increases.

One legal difference exists — state single-family/condo exemption The AB 1482 just-cause/rent-cap exemption for single-family homes & condos is unavailable if the property is owned by a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with at least one corporate member (and the statutory notice must be given). Corporate-owned non-controlled SFDs/condos therefore stay under the state cap; natural persons may qualify for the exemption. Corporate vs individual (only for units outside local rent control) sf.gov — AB 1482

Academic context NON-GOVERNMENT SOURCE

Peer-reviewed research on San Francisco's 1994 rent-control expansion (Diamond, McQuade & Qian, Who Pays for Rent Control?) reports that 91.45% of San Francisco landlords were individuals and corporate landlords managed 8.55% of multifamily housing in their sample, and that corporate landlords were more likely to reduce rent-controlled supply after the law changed. This is an academic paper — not an official government source — so, per the verification policy, the figures are not used as verified values in the tables; see F-5.

Individual owners in San Francisco — normal practice (synthesis of official rules)

  • Report units yearly (deadline March 1) → receive the rent-increase license.
  • Apply the current annual rate on each tenancy's anniversary, adding any properly banked years.
  • Serve a 30-day (or 90-day) written notice with the required content.
  • File a documented petition only if pursuing O&M/capital/utility/comparable-rent increases.
  • Keep rent ledgers and notices — tenants can challenge increases at the Rent Board.

Sources: Housing Inventory, rent increases, petitions, tenant resources.
