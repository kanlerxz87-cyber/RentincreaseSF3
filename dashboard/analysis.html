Finding 2 — Raise one unit or both? (discrimination analysis)

Answer: raise both, under one uniform written policy — do not single out one unit, and do not force the percentages to match.

  • No law requires identical increases across units. SF rules compute each tenancy's rent individually (its own base rent, anniversary and banked history) — see the per-tenant mechanics in sf.gov — Learn about rent increases. Different outcomes are normal and lawful when they come from different rent histories.
  • Different treatment becomes a legal risk only when the real driver is a protected characteristic. Federal: differing “terms, conditions, or privileges” of rental because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status or national origin is unlawful (42 U.S.C. § 3604(b)). San Francisco adds protected classes (age, disability, family status, source of income, sexual orientation, gender identity, pregnancy, etc.) — sf.gov — housing discrimination. Complaints can go to the SF Human Rights Commission, the state Civil Rights Department, or HUD.
  • Occupant changes must never drive the amount. Unit A's occupancy changed (one original tenant remains; one occupant replaced). The Rent Board is explicit that a landlord “cannot charge additional rent simply because a new (or additional) roommate has moved in” — sf.gov. Tie every dollar to the rate table and rent ledger only.
  • Raising only one unit, without a documented basis, invites exactly the inquiry you want to avoid (unequal treatment claims, or a demand to explain the disparity). Raising both with identical procedure — and letting the arithmetic produce the different percentages — is the defensible, normal-course approach.
  • Do not artificially equalize. Charging Unit B only 1.6% “to be fair” isn't required by law and abandons lawful entitlement; charging Unit A 13.1% “to match” would violate its cap. Fairness = same rules applied to each tenancy's own history.
  • Document the rationale in one sentence per unit: “Annual allowable increase under the SF Rent Ordinance (rate in effect on effective date) plus banked increases for skipped years [list years].” No reference to tenants, occupants, complaints or market rent.

Finding 3 — Would it pass an SF tenant lawyer's review?

A tenant-side reviewer (who can be funded for free — Eviction Defense Collaborative / Right to Counsel, Rent Board counseling; sf.gov resources) will test the following. The recommended approach for both units is built to satisfy every line:

  • Coverage confirmed — pre-6/13/1979 building, no exemption applies (F-3/F-7 open items for the specific address).
  • License first — Housing Inventory reported; rent increase license active on the effective date (sf.gov).
  • Correct rate for the effective date — 1.6% for effective dates through Feb 28, 2027 (Current Rates / Form 571).
  • Once per 12 months — no second increase inside 12 months; anniversary logic correct (sf.gov).
  • Banked math auditable — per-year rates from Form 571, added not compounded, no rounding up, each skipped year documented (sf.gov banking).
  • Notice timing — 30 days (≤10%) / 90 days (>10%), +5 if mailed (Civ. Code § 827).
  • Notice content — dollar amount, percentage, effective date, banked portion/years; rate used is the one in effect on the effective date (sf.gov).
  • No occupant-based charges — increase independent of the 2026 occupancy change on Unit A (sf.gov).
  • Uniform policy, both units — same template, same standards, differences only from rent history (Finding 2; § 3604(b)).
  • Neutral documented rationale — “Ordinance annual + banked increases,” dated ledger, copy of notice and license kept.
  • No pending dispute coloring the timing — if repair requests or tenant petitions exist, keep the increase tied to the anniversary calendar and note it in the file.
  • Conditional items disclosed — Unit B computed only after rent-history verification (F-1); pre-3/2027 effective dates avoid the unpublished rate (F-2).
Bottom line: a review under these steps would conclude the increases follow the Rent Ordinance's own published arithmetic, use the standard notice and licensing process, contain no tenant-specific or protected-class factors, and are applied consistently across units — i.e., normal business practice in San Francisco rent control.
