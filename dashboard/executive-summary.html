1. Coverage — both units are rent controlled

The building was constructed in 1940. Residential units built on or before June 13, 1979 are covered by the San Francisco Rent Ordinance (rent control and just-cause eviction protection), unless an exemption applies (e.g., single-family dwelling, subsidized unit). Coverage should be confirmed for the specific address with the Rent Board / Property Information Map — FLAG F-3 (sf.gov — Learn about SF rental laws).

Because both units are under local rent control, the state Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482) rent cap does not apply — units whose local ordinance restricts increases to less than 5% + CPI are exempt from the state cap, and the local rules remain in force (sf.gov — AB 1482). Federal law sets no rent ceiling; its relevance is anti-discrimination (Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. § 3604) — Legal Framework.

2. What is “reasonable and normal” in San Francisco

  • Normal increase = the Rent Board annual allowable rate + any banked (skipped) annual increases, imposed once every 12 months. Current rate: 1.6% for increases effective Mar 1, 2026 – Feb 28, 2027 (60% of Bay Area CPI-U) (sf.gov — Learn about rent increases, Form 571).
  • Annual/banked increases need no petition (Form 571: imposed “without filing a petition”), but do need a rent-increase license (Housing Inventory) and a written notice: 30 days if ≤ 10%, 90 days if > 10% (Cal. Civ. Code § 827(b); sf.gov — Banked rent increases).
  • Bigger increases are possible only via documented Rent Board petitions (O&M up to 7%, capital improvements, utility passthroughs, special circumstances) — Procedures & Notices.

4. Raise one unit or both?

Recommendation: apply the same written policy to both units — each unit's full lawful entitlement (annual + banked), same notice template, same service standards. Nothing in SF law requires identical percentages across units; the Rent Ordinance computes each tenancy's rent individually (base rent, anniversary, banked history). Different outcomes that flow entirely from rent history are objectively justified; picking one unit arbitrarily, or tying an increase to tenant composition (e.g., a new occupant), is what invites discrimination scrutiny. Federal law bans different terms “because of” protected characteristics (42 U.S.C. § 3604(b)), and San Francisco protects additional classes (sf.gov — housing discrimination). Details: Analysis & Findings.

5. Scrutiny readiness (SF tenant-lawyer review)

An SF tenant lawyer will check: unit coverage; a current rent-increase license; the correct rate for the effective date; the once-per-12-months limit; correct, non-compounded, non-rounded banked math; 30/90-day written notice with dollar amount, percentage, effective date and banked breakdown; no charges for additional occupants; and a neutral, documented business rationale. Free tenant-side lawyers exist and are easy to reach (Eviction Defense Collaborative / Right to Counsel, Rent Board counseling, tenant counselors — sf.gov — Tenant and Landlord Resources), so any increase should be built to pass that review from day one.
