Executive summary

Two rent-controlled units in a 1940 two-unit building in ZIP 94122 are occupied. Unit 1 was re-let on 1 February 2025 at $3,995 and is now at $3,995 after a tenancy change on 1 April 2026. Unit 2 has been at $3,150 since 1 June 2019 with no increase recorded. The question is what an increase may lawfully be, how to serve it, and whether one unit or both should receive one.

  1. The ceiling is local, not state. The Rent Board's allowable annual increase for 1 March 2026 – 28 February 2027 is 1.6% of base rent. The prior period was 1.4%. The Attorney General's 8.8% figure for the San Francisco area is the state cap and does not apply, because Civil Code 1947.12(d)(3) yields to a stricter local ordinance.
  2. Unit 1: three lawful outcomes. 1.4% ($55.93, to $4,050.93) if the 1 February 2026 anniversary was used; 1.6% ($63.92, to $4,058.92) for an effective date on or after 1 March 2026; or 3.0% ($119.85, to $4,114.85) at the 1 February 2027 anniversary if the 1.4% was banked and not used.
  3. Unit 2: 13.1% is the accumulated maximum, and it is the big number. Seven annual allowances from 1 June 2020 to 1 June 2026 — 1.8, 0.7, 2.3, 3.6, 1.7, 1.4, 1.6 — add to 13.1% without compounding. On $3,150 that is $412.65, taking rent to $3,562.65. Because 13.1% exceeds 10%, 90 days' written notice is required (95 if mailed). This is the most error-prone step in the file.
  4. Three prerequisites come before any notice. A current rent increase licence for each unit (Ordinance 37.15(e)); a 30/90-day written notice stating dollar amount, percentage and effective date, with the banked portion identified; and no retaliation flag in the preceding 180 days.
  5. One formal defect voids the whole increase. Ordinance 37.3(b)(5) and Rule 4.10(b) make a non-conforming increase null and void, with only a narrow good-faith allowance of 0.5% of prior base rent. The notice wording matters more than the percentage.
  6. One unit or both: raise both, with one method and a written basis for each number. Nothing reviewed requires the two units to receive the same increase, and the two calculations are different by design — different anniversary dates, different banked histories. The discrimination exposure is not the increase you serve; it is an unexplained difference between two units. Federal disparate-impact liability does not require intent, so the defence is a uniform rule, a uniform template, and a documented calculation for each unit. Withholding a lawful increase is not itself unlawful — the unused percentage simply banks — but it converts a current decision into a deferred one with a larger number and a 90-day notice requirement later.
  7. No rent increase may be justified by the April 2026 occupancy change. Adding an occupant is not a lawful basis for an increase, and the market-rate 6.14 / Costa-Hawkins route is closed while an original tenant remains in occupancy.
  8. 3 values in this review are unverified, 4 more depend on facts that were not supplied, and 8 need a judgement call. Each is published on the Flagged for review page and none blocks the rest of the analysis. The open facts that matter most: licence status for both units, whether a 1 February 2026 increase was actually served, the 2019 rent history supporting the $3,150 base, and any repair or complaint history in the last 180 days. No value has been inferred for any of them.

How this was built

  • Every statement on every page is a row in a machine-readable registry (data/findings.json) and must point to an entry in the source register (data/sources.json). The build refuses to run if a row points at a missing source, a non-HTTPS URL, or a host that is not an official government domain or an SEC filing.
  • Each source was retrieved and read directly on 2026-11-06. Where a page was a summary rather than the legal text, it is labelled official summary; where the legal text itself was read, it is labelled primary source; where the document is a company filing, it is labelled issuer filing.
  • Where a value could not be confirmed from an official source it is blank and marked Unverified. Nothing has been estimated, averaged, or inferred to fill a gap.
  • Where two official sources disagree, and where a conclusion is judgement rather than a quoted prohibition, the row is marked Flag and reproduced on the Flagged for review page.
  • Secondary sources — law-firm blogs, brokerage calculators, listing sites — were located during research and were not used to support any row. Several of them disagreed with each other (1.6% vs 1.7% for the same period), which is why the Rent Board's own documents are the anchor.
