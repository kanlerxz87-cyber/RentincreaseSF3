One unit or both?

Whether to raise one unit or both, tested against fair-housing, retaliation and Rent Ordinance rules - and what documentation makes either choice defensible.

The question

Is it acceptable to raise the rent on one unit, or is it safer to raise both so that no discrimination claim can be made?

Short answer: neither choice is required by law, and neither is automatically safer. What protects the owner is a written, objective rule applied consistently and documented before it is applied. A difference in outcome is lawful when it follows from a difference in facts - here, seven years of skipped increases in Unit B versus two in Unit A. A difference in treatment between similarly placed tenants is what invites inquiry.

Nothing in the ordinance or the cited statutes requires equal percentage or equal dollar increases, and no official source requires an owner to raise rent on every unit. The risk management is evidentiary, not numerical.

How to document it in one page

  1. Write the rule before serving anything: for example, "each year the owner applies the Rent Board's annual allowable increase on each unit's anniversary date, and takes banked years where at least 24 months have passed without an increase." S2 S3
  2. Record each unit's tenancy start, base rent, and every prior increase (date, percentage, dollar amount, notice and proof of service). S11
  3. Show the calculation for each unit, including the rate period used. S4
  4. Serve both notices on the same schedule, using the same template, so the only difference between them is the arithmetic. S14
  5. If a decision is made to raise one unit and not the other, write down the reason (for example: recent hardship, a repair dispute being resolved, a pending lease change) and keep it. A reason recorded at the time is evidence; a reason reconstructed later is not.
  6. Never make a protected characteristic, a complaint, or a request for repairs part of the reasoning. S18 S19 S20

If a tenant raises it

The Rent Board does not adjudicate discrimination, harassment or retaliation; those claims go to court or another forum. S10 The Rent Board does adjudicate whether the amount was lawful, at no charge and with no time limit. S11 That split matters: the arithmetic file answers the Rent Board; the written rule and the consistency record answer the discrimination question.
