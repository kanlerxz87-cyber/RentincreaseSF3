Flagged for review

Everything on this page is either (a) unconfirmed because no official source settles it, or (b) a place where two official sources disagree. Nothing here has been guessed and filled in; where a value could not be established it is left blank on the page that would have used it.

Severity key — High changes a number a tenant would be asked to pay · Medium changes timing or documents · Low presentation or provenance.

F-1 · Banking: the 24-month rule versus a 12-month worked example High

Two official sf.gov pages do not agree on when a skipped increase becomes banked.

  • The Banking page: “A skipped increase is only banked if at least 24 full months pass without any rent increase. If less than 24 months pass, the skipped increase is lost…”
  • The “Learn about rent increases” page, worked example: an owner who skipped the 1 June 2024 increase may bank it because “12 full months has elapsed since the skipped rent increase first became available”, and a different increase is “permanently lost” because 12 months had not elapsed.
  • A third page, “Annual rent increase for multiple years”, is written for increases “exactly two or more years” since the last increase, which is consistent with 24 months.

Effect on this file: none on the amounts. Unit 1 has 24 months of no increase by 1 February 2027 (lease 1 Feb 2025), and Unit 2 has had none since 1 June 2019. Both ledgers pass on either reading. The conservative single-year alternative for Unit 1 (1.6% = $63.92 → $4,058.92) is shown as a fallback for anyone who prefers to rely only on the current year.

Sources: Banking page · worked examples · multiple years. Would be closed by: a Rent Board counselor's written confirmation, or the text of Rent Board Rule 4.12.

F-2 · Unit 2 rent history High

13.1% / $412.65 → $3,562.65 assumes that no rent increase has been imposed at any anniversary since the lease began on 1 June 2019. No lease, notice, receipt or proof of service was supplied for Unit 2, and the owner's statement records only the 1 June 2019 rent of $3,150.00.

Effect: the 13.1% ceiling and the $412.65 figure are conditional. If any increase was taken, the corresponding row drops out of the ledger on Units & math and the ceiling falls by that percentage. The 10.0% recommendation is affected only if the true ceiling is below 10%.

Sources: banking rules · rate table · the rent-history evidence a tenant would rely on. Would be closed by: the oldest available lease, prior notices, and bank or payment records for the tenancy.

F-3 · The 2027–28 allowance is not published yet Medium

The Rent Board publishes the annual allowance each 1 March. The rate for 1 March 2027 – 29 February 2028 does not exist in any official source as of 7 November 2026, so no page on this site quotes it.

Effect: every figure here uses only published rates. A Unit 2 increase effective after 28 February 2027 could lawfully include the new rate in addition — but the amount is left blank rather than estimated.

Sources: § 37.3(a)(1) · latest announcement.

F-4 · Unit 1's anniversary after the April 2026 renewal Medium

The anniversary follows the date the tenancy began (1 February 2025), not the date of a later renewal, so the ledger uses 1 February. The residual doubt is documentary: the current lease is dated 1 April 2026 at the same rent, and if the owner treats 1 April as a new rental agreement the anniversary could be argued to be 1 April.

Effect: the 3.0% figure is the same either way; only the earliest effective date moves (1 April 2027 instead of 1 February 2027). The site uses 1 February because the tenancy is a continuing tenancy with the same original tenant in possession, and because a new occupant joining cannot reset the tenancy or support extra rent.

Sources: anniversary and additional-occupant rules. Would be closed by: the signed 1 April 2026 document and a counselor's view on whether it restated or replaced the tenancy.

F-5 · No official rent benchmark for this address or ZIP code Medium

Nothing on this site states a market rent, a neighbourhood average, or a “typical” increase in dollars. The Zip code was searched for an official published rent level and none was found in the sources reviewed, so the corresponding rows are blank.

Effect: the questions “how much would the market bear” and “what do neighbouring owners charge” are not answered here at all. They cannot be answered from an official source.

F-6 · No official comparison of corporate and individual owner behaviour Low

The site describes institutional practice only where the official record documents it — the Rent Ordinance's findings on algorithmic pricing and the federal antitrust case. It does not quantify how often large owners versus small owners take increases, because no official dataset was located.

Effect: the “corporate vs individual” answer on Reasonable? is qualitative and labelled as analysis wherever it reasons beyond the quoted text.

Sources: § 37.10C · DOJ.

F-7 · Property facts are the owner's statement Medium

Year built (1940), unit count (two), and the absence of a separately sold single-family home or condominium are all taken from the owner's description. No certificate of occupancy, assessor record or City permit history was examined. Coverage under the Rent Ordinance was inferred from the building year, which is how the City directs owners to check.

Effect: if the certificate of occupancy were issued after 13 June 1979 the unit would fall outside rent control entirely and the analysis would change.

Sources: sf.gov — coverage and how to check · SF Property Information Map. Would be closed by: the City's property record for the parcel.

F-8 · Rent increase license status unknown High

This site cannot see the Rent Board Portal, so it cannot confirm that either unit has a current rent increase license. The rule is absolute: without one on the effective date, “your rent increase notice will not be enforceable”.

Effect: every recommendation on this site is conditional on that check.

Sources: sf.gov — Housing Inventory and license · § 37.3(a)(1). Would be closed by: logging into portal.sfrb.org and printing the license status for both units.

F-9 · A 13.1% increase effective 1 February 2027 is already out of reach Medium

Civ. Code § 827 requires 90 days' notice for an increase above 10%, plus 5 days if mailed. Counted back from 1 February 2027 that is 3 November 2026 for personal service and 29 October 2026 by mail — both before today's date. A February 2027 effective date for Unit 2's maximum would therefore fail on notice, even though the amount is lawful.

Effect: the site recommends 1 June 2027 for Unit 2 (serve by 3 March 2027, or 26 February 2027 by mail) and flags the earlier editions' phrasing “serve by 30 November 2026” as correct only for a 28 February 2027 effective date served in person (25 November 2026 by mail).

Sources: § 827 · CCP § 1013 · deadline planner.

F-10 · Pending File 261020 — status differs from earlier editions Medium

File 261020 (“Administrative Code – Cap On Rent Increases for Rent-Controlled Units”) would cap combined banked and capital-improvement increases at 10% a year for three years. It was introduced on 15 September 2026 and assigned under the 30-day rule. The Legistar record's current Status field reads Pending Committee Action — earlier editions of this repository described the status as “30 Day Rule”, which is the introductory action, not the current status.

Effect: it is not law, so it does not change any ceiling here. It is the reason a 10.0% first step is recommended for Unit 2 rather than 13.1%. Re-check before serving any banked notice.

Source: Legistar File 261020 (retrieved 7 November 2026).

F-11 · Housing subsidy status unknown High

Neither tenancy's subsidy status is known. It matters in three ways: Housing Choice Voucher and HOPWA tenancies are subject to the annual limits only where the base rent exceeds the Housing Authority's payment standard; capital improvement, bond, utility and operating-cost passthroughs are not permitted for those tenancies at all; and Section 8 Certificate tenancies are exempt from the rent increase provisions because the rent is set by HUD.

Effect: if either tenancy is subsidised the ceiling may not be the 1.6%-plus-banked figure used here.

Sources: sf.gov — Partial Exemption for Certain Subsidized Rental Units · AB 1482 page (deed-restricted and subsidised housing exemptions). Would be closed by: confirming with each tenant whether a voucher is in use, with the Housing Authority if so.

F-12 · Two official worked examples carry date labels that conflict with the rate table Low

The “Learn about rent increases” page contains two worked examples whose arithmetic is right but whose rate-year labels do not match the Rent Board's own table:

  • “A tenant moved in on June 1, 2025… the owner wants to increase the tenant's rent for June 1, 2026. The annual allowable increase in effect on June 1, 2025 is 1.6%.” The allowance effective on 1 June 2025 is 1.4%; 1.6% applies from 1 March 2026.
  • The banking example refers to “the annual allowable rent increase in effect on August 1, 2024 (1.4%)” while the increase under discussion is effective 1 August 2025 — 1.4% is the 1 March 2025 – 28 February 2026 rate.

Effect: this site uses document 571 for every rate and treats the examples as illustrations of method only. Anyone reading the examples literally could overstate a 2025 increase by 0.2 percentage points.

Sources: worked examples · controlling rate table.
