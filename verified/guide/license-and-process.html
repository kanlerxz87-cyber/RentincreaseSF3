The Rent Board: license, reporting and the increase process

You read that you must “inform the Rent Board and apply for a license” to stop the increase being challenged. That is broadly right, and it applies to you as an individual owner — but the mechanics are different from what “apply for a license” suggests. Here is what each step actually is, with the official text and a link for every one.

Direct answers

Your questionAnswerSource
As an individual owner (not a corporation), must I report to the Rent Board? Yes. “All owners of residential property are required to report.” “Most owners are required to do this even if you are not a landlord.” sf.gov
Do I need a license before notifying tenants of an increase? Yes. “A landlord must have a current rent increase license on file with the Rent Board before any annual or banked rent increase can go into effect.” The notice “will not be enforceable” without it on the effective date. sf.gov
Is there an application form or a fee for the license? No. The license is “automatically generated for non-owner occupied units” once the Housing Inventory report is submitted. “Reporting is free.” sf.gov
Does a 2-unit building count? Yes. Reporting requirements began 1 March 2023 “for condominiums and units in buildings with fewer than 10 units.” sf.gov
When is the deadline? 1 March, every year. Late submissions still work; there is no monetary penalty for filing after the deadline. sf.gov
Must I file the increase notice itself with the Rent Board? No. For annual and banked increases: “Landlords are not required to file a petition with the City to impose these types of rent increases.” Only six other increase types need a petition. sf.gov · sf.gov
Does reporting exempt the Rent Board Fee? No. “Reporting into the Rent Board Housing Inventory does not request an exemption of the Rent Board Fee.” sf.gov
Is the information I submit private? No. “The information entered in the housing inventory submission is public,” including name, business contact information, square footage, bedroom/bathroom counts and the vacancy/occupancy history. It is submitted under penalty of perjury. sf.gov

Step by step: from nothing on file to a compliant increase

  1. Check coverage first. Your building is stated as built in 1940 with two units. Units built on or before 13 June 1979 that are not single-family dwellings generally have both rent control and eviction protection, so the Rent Ordinance applies. Confirm the build year on the City's Property Information Map and, if anything is unclear, ask a Rent Board counselor (415-252-4600). Source: sf.gov — Learn about San Francisco rental laws
  2. Create a Rent Board Portal account and open the Housing Inventory. Source: sf.gov — The Rent Board Housing Inventory · portal: portal.sfrb.org
  3. Report each unit. For a tenant-occupied unit the portal asks for: your name and business contact information (or your manager's); a business registration number if any; approximate square footage in 250 sq ft increments; number of bedrooms; number of bathrooms; the date current occupancy began; and the base rent in $250 increments, plus whether the rent includes utilities. Use the “Individual Unit” form rather than the bulk spreadsheet — bulk upload is only for properties with five or more units. Source: sf.gov — The Rent Board Housing Inventory
  4. Confirm the license was generated for each tenant-occupied unit. The license “will be automatically generated for non-owner occupied units” after submission. Note that tenants can also look your license up on the portal, so this is the first thing a tenant's advocate will check. Source: sf.gov — The Rent Board Housing Inventory
  5. Do this before the notice takes effect, not before you write it. The statutory test is a current license “on the effective date of the notice”. The license “does not need to be included with your rent increase notice”. Source: sf.gov — The Rent Board Housing Inventory
  6. Reconstruct each tenancy's rent history. Anniversary date, every increase served and the date of each, and the rent charged at the start of each 12-month period. San Francisco allows one increase per 12 months, and the date of an increase becomes the new anniversary date. Source: sf.gov — Learn about rent increases in San Francisco
  7. Compute the ceiling. Sum the applicable annual percentages for each skipped year (never compound), multiply by the current base rent (base rent only, including parking/storage, excluding passthroughs), and do not round up. If only part is used, the rest is banked. Source: sf.gov — Banked rent increases · Rent Board document 571
  8. Check the pending ordinance before choosing the amount. File 261020 would cap combined banked and capital-improvement increases at 10% a year for three years. Status on 7 November 2026: Pending Committee Action — not law, so it does not change today's ceiling, but it is the reason a 10% step is the safer Unit 2 choice. Source: Legistar File 261020 · flagged as F-10
  9. Serve the statutory notice. 30 days if the increase is 10% or less; 90 days if more; add 5 days if served by mail. Use the rate in effect on the effective date, not the date of service. Source: Civ. Code § 827 · sf.gov
  10. Keep the file. Notice copy, proof of service, the Housing Inventory confirmation, the license, and the rent history. A tenant may petition the Rent Board for free, with no filing deadline, to challenge an unlawful increase or to have the lawful rent determined. Source: sf.gov — Report an unlawful rent increase · sf.gov — Tenant Petitions

Analysis Because the license status and the occupancy history are public, and because a tenant petition costs nothing and has no deadline, the practical exposure from skipping the reporting step is not a fine — it is that the whole increase is unenforceable and recoverable. Fix the report first.

There is no charge for filing a Rent Board petition, in either direction.

One thing to check before you rely on any of this: if either tenancy receives a housing subsidy, the ceiling can change. Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8) and HOPWA tenancies are subject to the just-cause provisions, the annual rent increase limitations apply only where the base rent exceeds the Housing Authority's payment standard, and capital-improvement, bond, utility and operating-cost passthroughs are not permitted at all for those tenancies; Section 8 Certificate tenancies are exempt from the rent increase provisions because the rent is set by HUD. sf.gov — Partial Exemption for Certain Subsidized Rental Units.
