1. The Rent Board question, answered first

You are an individual owner, not a corporation. You still have to report to the Rent Board and hold a rent-increase licence — but you do not need the Board's approval of the amount, and you do not file the increase notice with the Board.

There are exactly two Rent Board obligations that bear on a rent increase, and they are both reporting obligations under Administrative Code §37.15:

  1. Report into the Housing Inventory by 1 March every year. All owners of residential property must do this, including owners who are individuals and including owners who do not currently let a unit. Buildings with 1–9 residential units and condominiums have been in scope since 1 March 2023; buildings with 10 or more units since 1 July 2022. This two-unit building is in the first group.
  2. Make sure the resulting rent-increase licence is current on the effective date of the increase. When you report a unit as tenant-occupied, the licence is generated automatically — there is no separate application and no fee. The licence is not an approval of the percentage.

If you report late, you can still file, and you will still get the licence. But an increase whose effective date falls while you are unlicensed does not have to be paid: §37.15(e) suspends the licence during non-compliance and says the tenant is not obligated to pay the increased rent for the months of suspension, and sf.gov warns that a notice taking effect while unlicensed may be null and void, potentially for more than one year.

sf.gov — Rent Board Housing Inventory · Admin. Code §37.15 (sf.gov) · sf.gov — Notice of Owner Noncompliance · sf.gov — Learn about rent increases

Practical consequence: report first, confirm the licence, then serve the notice. Nothing else about the Rent Board needs to happen before you notify tenants.

2. What is "reasonable and normal" here

For a rent-controlled unit in San Francisco the answer is not a matter of judgement. The lawful ceiling is the normal figure, because the ordinance sets it citywide at 60% of the Bay Area CPI change, capped at 7%:

4. How to inform tenants, and what the reason may be

The reason for an annual or banked increase does not have to be justified to the tenant: it is the ordinance formula. What matters is that the reason is not a prohibited one, and that the notice is correct. The owner's written reason should be the same for both units.

ElementRequirementSource

5. Would a tenant-side lawyer accept it?

San Francisco tenants have access to free counsel, and an increase will be tested on procedure before it is tested on policy. An increase that satisfies all of the following is ordinary business practice and should survive that review. Each test is set out with its source on Tenant-lawyer test.

  1. Licence current on the effective date, provable from the Rent Board Portal.
  2. Amount at or below the published allowance, plus only correctly computed banking, on base rent only.
  3. At least 12 months since the last increase, on the correct anniversary date.
  4. Notice of the right length, with the four required elements, served.
  5. No protected characteristic, complaint or added occupant anywhere in the reasoning.
  6. One written policy applied to both units, with any difference explained by tenancy history.

6. One unit or both?

Raise both, on the same written policy. Raising a single unit is not automatically unlawful, but it removes the simplest answer to a discrimination or retaliation inquiry. If both units are treated by the same written rule — the published allowance plus documented banking, applied on each unit's own anniversary — then any difference in outcome is explained by objective tenancy history rather than by who the tenants are. If only one unit is raised, record the objective reason in writing and keep it with the file.
